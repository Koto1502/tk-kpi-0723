The no-ads sale packs

Did v28 remove them, and what would removing them cost?
tiny-knightfall-idle-rpg-game · GA4 analytics_538412813 + live Play storefront · generated 2026-09-18

They were not removed. Two new builds did ship — v28 on 2026-09-16 and v29 on 2026-09-17 — but players on both are still opening and completing purchases of skip.ad.permanent.sale, most recently 2026-09-18. All three .sale SKUs are still ACTIVE / AVAILABLE on the live Play storefront, and the 2026-09-15 export from game source still specifies them.

And they are worth far more than they look. Ad-removal SKUs are 36.7% of all tracked IAP revenue; the sale variants alone are 24.6%. Over the last 28 days permanent.sale by itself was 22% of total revenue. Removing it is a bet on roughly a fifth of the game's income.

2 · The packs are still being sold on those builds

Four independent signals can answer this, and they are not equally strong. purchase_click and iap_purchase_success are client custom events — good evidence the shop still shows the card, but in principle a stale logging call could outlive the UI. in_app_purchase is the Firebase SDK auto-log from Play Billing — it fires on the billing result regardless of what the client shop is displaying, so on its own it cannot prove the offer exists. The decisive one is earn: earn.position carries the SKU id for shop grants, so it is the client actually paying out the reward.

Two ways the data could have lied here, both ruled out. Mis-stamped versions (a purchase started before the update and logged after it): no — all nine users involved appear on exactly one build, with first_open on that same build, so none of them straddles an update. QA devices: no — they are nine separate installs in nine countries (Switzerland, Spain, Poland, United States ×2, Germany, Indonesia, South Korea, Hong Kong).

One free win: skip.ad.annual.sale has never been tapped or bought once in the entire export, despite being active on the storefront. If the goal is pruning dead sale SKUs, that is the genuinely dead one — and it costs nothing to remove.

4 · Why "they'll just buy the $29.99 one" is unlikely

This price point already moved once. At v21 (2026-08-08) the offer mix flipped from mostly-$29.99 to mostly-$10.99: revenue for the $29.99 option fell ~70% per capita, but combined buyers per 1,000 users rose, 9.3 → 10.5. That is price expanding the market, not cannibalising it — the $10.99 buyers were largely people who were never going to pay $29.99. No user has ever bought both SKUs.

So the question is what share of sale buyers would re-convert at 2.7× the price. Applied to the last 28 days ($1,223 across ~111 purchases):

More than ~37% of sale buyers must convert at the higher price just to break even, and the v21 episode points the other way. This is arithmetic on a bracket, not a forecast — but it frames the bet honestly, and the downside is a fifth of total revenue.

5 · Refunds — and the reason this SKU may deserve removal after all

GA4 has no refund event, so everything above is gross. The Play Developer API does have them: purchases.voidedpurchases.list gives the voided orders and orders.get turns each into a SKU and an amount. Play only serves a 30-day rolling window — it rejects any earlier start — so this is a running ledger, not a backfill.

In that window skip.ad.permanent.sale is the single most-refunded SKU in the game, 7 of 18 refunds:

The reasons are the finding, not the rate. Across the full ledger, permanent.sale's refunds are 4× not received, 2× defective, 1× undocumented code 8 — almost all of them players saying they paid and did not receive it, or that it was broken. Every other refunded SKU in the window shows ordinary noise instead: accidental purchase, friendly fraud, chargeback. A 6.7% refund rate against 2.2% overall is elevated; a refund rate made of non-delivery claims is a different kind of problem.

Two readings fit, and this data cannot separate them:

  • A delivery bug on the sale SKU's grant path — players are charged and the entitlement never lands.
  • Refund abuse of a cheap permanent entitlement — buy permanent ad removal at $10.99, claim non-delivery, keep the benefit. "Not received" is the standard claim for this, and a permanent unlock is the natural target.

Tempting to point at the full-price twin, which shows 0 refunds on 14 purchases — but that is too few to mean anything (you would see zero about 37% of the time even at the same rate). Client-side grant coverage does not settle it either: it runs ~30% for permanent.sale against ~64% for permanent, but per-SKU grant coverage is a known instrumentation artifact in this app, ranging 9–73% for reasons that have nothing to do with delivery. Whether players actually receive the entitlement is a client and Play Console question, not a telemetry one — check the order status on one of these refunded orders against what the account was granted.

6 · A trap: do not read v28's ARPDAU as the effect

Over the same three days v28 shows roughly 4.5× v26.2's revenue per 1,000 DAU and ten times the ad-removal tap rate. None of that is caused by the release. Three days after a rollout, the people on the new build are the ones whose auto-update fired first — which correlates with opening the game daily, i.e. the engaged core, who are also the payers. Everyone still on v26.2 is the laggard tail. The comparison measures who updates fast, not what changed in the build.

A clean read needs install-date cohorts matched across builds, or simply waiting until v28/v29 reach majority adoption — about a week here, judging by v25 and v26.2.

7 · What to do

  • Answer the delivery question first — it decides everything else. Take one refunded permanent.sale order from the ledger, check in Play Console whether the purchase was acknowledged, and check whether that account holds the entitlement. If players genuinely are not receiving it, that is a bug to fix now and it outranks the pricing question entirely. If they are receiving it and refunding anyway, this is abuse of a cheap permanent unlock — and that is a real argument for removing or restructuring the SKU, independent of revenue.
  • Confirm the removal shipped at all. Four independent signals say it did not, including the client paying out the reward on v28 and v29. Check the submitted build before assuming the data is wrong.
  • Remove annual.sale now — zero taps, zero revenue, zero risk.
  • Do not delete permanent.sale purely on the pricing argument. It is ~22% of revenue and break-even needs ~37% of its buyers to re-convert at 2.7×. If the refunds turn out to be abuse, restructure it — a non-permanent tier, or a lower-value permanent unlock — rather than removing the price point that does the volume.
  • Wait a week before scoring any of it. Three days and ~500 devices cannot separate the change from update-speed selection.
  • Run the refund puller on a schedule. Play discards the window after 30 days, so refund history only exists if it is collected as it goes: uv run --with google-api-python-client --with google-auth python dashboard/pull_play_refunds.py.
Revenue is gross — this export has no refund or cancellation event, so reconcile against Play Console before booking any figure. It is also gross of VAT: Play bakes each country's VAT into the list price outside the US, and 75.4% of ad-removal revenue ($4,391 of $5,820) is billed in non-USD, so roughly a tenth of the headline is tax that never becomes your money. The share figures above are gross over a gross denominator, and the two permanent variants sell into a similar country mix, so this moves absolute dollars rather than the comparison that drives the break-even bracket. Only in_app_purchase carries money — every client-side event (purchase, purchase_click, iap_purchase_success, first_purchase) has no value, price or currency field, so there is no second revenue signal inside GA4 to reconcile against. SKU comes from event_params.product_id; the items array is empty in this app. Only events_intraday_* shards exist, so the current day is always partial.
